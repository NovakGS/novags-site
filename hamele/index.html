Why Fly in Winter

Leaf-Off Is the Best Time to Plan Next Season

From November through March, the hardwoods are bare and the property is quiet. That window is when a mapping flight sees the most ground, disturbs the least, and leaves the most time to act on what it finds before spring green-up.

See the Ground Through the TimberBare hardwoods let the camera reach the forest floor. Trails, old logging roads, fence lines, wet spots, and blowdowns show up that summer canopy hides completely.
Conifers Stand OutEvergreen cover is easy to separate from bare timber in winter imagery, so thermal cover and screening can be mapped block by block.
Nothing to DisturbFlying after the season means no pressure on deer during the hunt. Cold, still mornings are also when thermal contrast is strongest.
Time to Act on ItMaps in hand by January leave the rest of the winter for cutting trails, hinge-cutting, laying out food plots, and choosing stand sites before spring.

The Combination

Why Public LiDAR and Drone Mapping Work Better Together

LiDAR and drone mapping answer different questions. Each covers the other's weak spot, and putting them on the same vertical datum lets one check the other.

Public LiDAR Airborne laser, 2024

  • Laser pulses reach the ground through gaps in the canopy, so the bare-earth model holds up under heavy timber.
  • Measures tree height directly, which is how canopy and cover classes were built here.
  • Free, county-wide, and consistent from one property to the next.
  • A few years old. It doesn't show this year's cuts, plots, or blowdowns.
  • No photo, and a coarser ground grid (2 ft here).

Drone Mapping Matrice 4E & 4T, Nov 2026

  • Current to the day of the flight.
  • A sharp aerial photo, far more detailed than satellite imagery.
  • Adds thermal from the same visit with the 4T.
  • A camera can't see through dense canopy, so the ground model is estimated under thick cover.
  • In leaf-off hardwoods, bare branches don't reconstruct, so the drone surface can't measure tree height.

Together

LiDAR supplies the ground and the trees. The drone supplies today's picture and the thermal. On this parcel the leaf-off drone surface sat almost on the ground under the bare hardwoods. That is ideal for seeing the forest floor, and exactly why the LiDAR is used for canopy height. Before any analysis, both datasets were put in the same coordinate system and heights (NAVD88, US survey feet) and compared on open, flat ground.

See the Raw LiDAR

The 2024 LiDAR is 1.63 million laser points inside this frame, about 27 per square meter. Ground points are as classified in the county delivery. The rest are split by height above ground into low, mid, and high vegetation, so the timber reads crown by crown while the bare field shows as ground.

Terrain Guide

What Each Feature Is, and Why It Matters for Wildlife

Every color and line on the map above has a meaning. This guide explains each feature, what it tends to mean for deer and other wildlife, and how much of it is on this parcel. Tap Show on Map on any card to turn that layer on.

Landforms

The shape of the ground, classified from the LiDAR bare-earth model.

Knob / Hilltop

0.45 ac here

What It IsA high point where the ground falls away on every side.

Why It MattersDeer often bed just below the top, where they can watch downhill while rising daytime thermals carry scent up to them. Wind swirls around knobs, so approach and stand placement take care.

Ridge

1.47 ac here

What It IsA long, narrow spine of high ground.

Why It MattersRidges are natural travel routes. Deer tend to move just below the crest on the side away from the wind, where they stay off the skyline, can watch downhill, and still have the wind bringing them scent from over the top.

Point / Finger

1.92 ac here

What It IsA spur of high ground running off a ridge toward lower ground.

Why It MattersThe end of a point is classic bedding: a view down the slope, wind at the deer's back, and several escape routes. Fingers also channel travel up and down the hill.

Shoulder (Transition)

None here

What It IsThe rounded break where a ridge top rolls over into the slope.

Why It MattersShoulders are where flat high ground turns into hillside. Trails often follow them, and bedding commonly sits just over the break, out of sight from the top.

Sidehill

5.46 ac here

What It IsA steady slope between the high ground and the low ground.

Why It MattersDeer cross sidehills on trails that follow the contour lines. Steep sidehills are where benches form, and on gentle ground like this parcel, travel spreads out instead of funneling.

Bench

None here

What It IsA flat shelf partway up a slope.

Why It MattersBenches are easy walking and easy bedding on otherwise steep ground, so they often carry travel along the hill. This parcel's slopes are gentle (median about 6°), so none qualified. Steeper ground usually has several.

Draw / Bowl

2.18 ac here

What It IsA hollow or trough in the hillside that funnels toward lower ground.

Why It MattersDraws are sheltered from the wind and link the ridge to the bottom, so deer use them to move between bedding and food. In the evening and early morning, cooling air drains down them and carries scent downhill; once the sun warms the slopes, that flow reverses and rises.

Toe Slope (Transition)

None here

What It IsThe base of a slope where it flattens into the bottom.

Why It MattersTravel often runs along the toe of a hill, and the richer soil there can grow thicker browse.

Bottom / Drainage

1.34 ac here

What It IsLow ground along a draw or waterway.

Why It MattersBottoms collect water, browse, and thick cover. They are also where cold air pools on still mornings, so scent tends to settle and drift down them.

Depression

0.80 ac here

What It IsAn enclosed low spot with no outlet.

Why It MattersDepressions can hold seasonal water and stay wet after rain or snowmelt, which can attract wildlife and limit equipment access.

Travel Features

Drainage Lines

2,112 ft here

What It IsPaths surface water would follow, traced from the ground model. Not streams, and most are dry most of the year.

Why It MattersThey mark the draws and low ground that deer follow between ridge and field, and the crossings where trails come together.

Ridgelines

1,602 ft here

What It IsThe spines of the high ground, traced from the elevation model.

Why It MattersRidgelines show the travel routes along the high ground and where wind rolls over the top and eddies on the downwind side, a key to reading how scent moves.

Travel Hubs

8 here (5 draw, 3 ridge)

What It IsPlaces where three or more draws or ridges meet.

Why It MattersWhen several travel lines join, movement from different parts of the property passes through one spot. Hubs are worth a close look for stand and camera sites.

Saddle

1 here

What It IsA low dip in a ridge between two higher points.

Why It MattersDeer cross ridges at the lowest point to save energy, so saddles are natural funnels and long-time stand locations.

Cover & Edges

Classified from LiDAR canopy height plus the greenness of the leaf-off aerial.

Open / Field

4.76 ac here

What It IsGround where the vegetation is under 3 ft tall, such as fields, grass, and crops.

Why It MattersFood and visibility, but little security. Deer usually use open ground at low light and stay close to cover.

Thicket / Regrowth

0.35 ac here

What It IsYoung trees, shrubs, and brush from 3 to 15 ft tall.

Why It MattersThickets offer both browse and security cover, and they are favorite bedding areas, especially under hunting pressure. This parcel has very little.

Hardwood

8.06 ac here

What It IsDeciduous trees over 15 ft tall.

Why It MattersOaks and other hardwoods can drop mast such as acorns in the fall, but once leaves are down the understory opens up, and so does visibility.

Conifer

0.03 ac here

What It IsEvergreen timber that holds its needles through the winter.

Why It MattersConifers cut the wind, catch snow in their branches, and slow heat loss at night, so deer often shelter in them in hard winter weather. Adding or protecting conifer is a common habitat goal.

Hard Edge

1,081 ft here

What It IsAn abrupt line where open ground meets mature timber.

Why It MattersDeer step out at hard edges but have little cover to stage in. Softening a hard edge with shrubs or edge feathering (hinge-cutting along the timber line) creates a transition they can use.

Soft Edge

110 ft here

What It IsA gradual change from field to thicket to timber.

Why It MattersSoft edges offer browse and security next to the food, so deer tend to linger and stage in them before entering a field.

Inside Corner

1 here

What It IsA spot where the field cuts into the timber, so cover wraps around open ground.

Why It MattersDeer often enter a field at an inside corner, where they can step out with cover on two sides. Inside corners are classic stand sites on field edges.

Warmth, Water & Elevation

South-Facing Slopes

1.69 ac here

What It IsSlopes facing south or southwest, at 8 to 35°.

Why It MattersThey get the most winter sun, so they are warmer, melt off first, and green up first in spring. They are often preferred winter bedding.

Elevation & Contours

954–1,014 ft here

What It IsGround height colored from low to high, with lines every 2 ft and labeled lines every 10 ft.

Why It MattersContours show how steep the ground is and which way it faces at a glance. Tight lines mean steep slopes; wide lines mean flats. They are the base for planning trails, plots, and access.

Springs / Seeps

2 in the simulated example

What It IsGround water reaching the surface, usually at the head or side of a draw.

Why It MattersSpring water comes out of the ground at a steady temperature, warmer than winter air, so it stays open in winter. Open water and the early green growth around it draw deer.

Deer behavior shifts with weather, food, season, and hunting pressure. These are tendencies to plan around, not rules, and every feature should be confirmed on foot.

Informational, Not Real-Time

A thermal survey is a snapshot of one flight morning, delivered after processing. It gives a general picture of what uses the property and where animals were at that moment. It is not a real-time locating service and isn't meant for finding deer to hunt that day. Animals move, and deer under thick conifer cover or off the property at flight time won't be counted. Treat the numbers as a minimum.

From Flight to Hunting Map

  1. Leaf-Off Mapping FlightMatrice 4E mapping flight, processed into a high-detail aerial (orthomosaic) and surface model.
  2. Public LiDAR, Pulled and CheckedThe county's 2024 LiDAR tiles for the parcel are downloaded, merged into bare-earth and canopy models, and checked against the drone surface.

FAQ

Hunting Land Mapping Questions

Why fly hunting land in winter?

With the leaves down, a mapping flight sees through bare hardwoods to the ground: trails, old logging roads, fence lines, wet spots, and terrain that are hidden under summer canopy. Conifers stand out against the bare timber, and winter flights happen after the season, when there is nothing to disturb. The maps are in hand for spring work on food plots, trails, and stand sites.

Why combine public LiDAR with a drone flight?

Public LiDAR measures the bare ground and the height of the trees, even under heavy cover, but it is a few years old and has no photo. A drone flight is current and far more detailed, but a camera cannot see through dense canopy. Together they give a current, high-detail picture of the property on top of a reliable ground and canopy model, and each one checks the other.

Can a thermal flight tell me where the deer are right now?

No. A thermal survey is a snapshot of one flight morning, delivered after processing. It gives a general picture of wildlife presence and where animals were bedded or feeding at that time. It is informational only and is not a real-time locating service. Animals move, and deer under thick conifer cover or out of the flight area are not counted.

Was the thermal layer on this page flown on this property?

No. The thermal layer in this case study is a simulated example, built on this parcel's real terrain and cover to show the format of the deliverable. Everything else on the page comes from real data.

Can one flight do it all?

A Matrice 4T mapping flight can capture elevations, contours, and thermal scouting in one mission. This case study used the Matrice 4E for the most detailed map and the 4T for thermal.

Will the maps work in a hunting app?

Yes. Every layer comes in one Google Earth file (KMZ) that opens on a phone with a single tap. Terrain features, cover, edges, and contours also come as GPX and KML files for hunting apps such as onX, HuntStand, BaseMap, and Gaia GPS, along with a printed map and a live web map.

Is this a land survey?

No. The maps are produced from drone imagery and public LiDAR for planning and visualization. They are not a land survey and do not establish property boundaries.
