Before You Call

What to Do First

  • Don’t chase. A scared dog may run from anyone, even its own family, and many stop answering to their name. Chasing or loud calling can push a pet farther away.
  • Search close to home. Check under decks, porches, and sheds, inside garages, and under parked cars. Indoor cats that slip out often hide nearby and stay silent.
  • Report it to the shelter. Found strays in Dane County go to the Dane County Humane Society. File a lost pet report there, and check in person.
  • Call the microchip company. Mark your pet as missing so a scan at a vet or shelter reaches you.
  • Post it. Share a clear, recent photo in neighborhood groups and local lost pet pages, and put up flyers.
  • Note the last sighting. The time, the exact spot, and the direction your pet went decide where the search starts.
  • Talk to the neighbors. Permission to walk onto nearby yards and land lets you move in quickly once your pet is spotted.
  • Have the basics ready. A leash or carrier, strong-smelling treats, and the person your pet trusts most.

When Your Pet Is Spotted

A lost pet that is scared can bolt even from its owner. The goal is to let it come to you.

  • Move SlowlyWalk in quietly; don’t run or shout
  • Get LowSit or crouch, turn partly sideways, and avoid staring
  • Let It Come to YouToss a few treats and let your pet close the distance
  • No Sudden GrabsLeash or lift gently once your pet is calm and close

These are common tips from lost pet volunteers, not guarantees. Every pet is different.

FAA and Local Rules

Flown Legally, Day or Night

Every search is flown under FAA Part 107 by a certified remote pilot. Night searches follow the Part 107 night rules, including anti-collision lighting visible for three miles. Flights avoid passing over people.

  • Near the AirportMuch of Madison sits inside the controlled airspace around Dane County Regional Airport. Each flight there needs FAA authorization, which can cap the altitude, and a few spots near the runways can’t be flown at all.
  • ParksMany city, county, and state parks need a permit to launch a drone, or don’t allow it, so launches are planned from your property or another spot where takeoff is allowed.
  • WeatherHigh wind, rain, freezing fog, and icing keep the drone on the ground. Weather holds show on the live calendar.

FAQ

Lost Pet Recovery Questions

Does This Work for Cats?

Yes, though the odds are lower than for a dog in open ground. Cats are small and tend to tuck under decks, into brush, and into tight spaces a thermal camera can't see into. A search can still cover fields, woodlines, and rooftops quickly, and it works best after dark, when a warm body stands out most against cool ground.

When Does a Thermal Search Work Best?

After dark, early in the morning, and on cool, overcast days. Sun warms roofs, pavement, rocks, and bare ground, which can hide a pet's heat. Cold, clear nights give the strongest contrast. Rain, fog, and heavy snow cut down what the camera can see, and high wind or freezing conditions can keep the drone on the ground.

Will the Drone Scare My Pet?

The search is flown from well above the ground to keep noise down. The drone moves closer only as far as needed to confirm a match on the zoom camera. If a pet starts moving away, the drone backs off and keeps it in view from a distance.

What Else Shows Up on Thermal?

Deer, coyotes, foxes, raccoons, and other people's pets all give off heat. Every heat signature is checked on the zoom camera before it is called a match, so no one is sent after the wrong animal.

Is Finding My Pet Guaranteed?

No. Roofs, decks, culverts, dense evergreens, and leaf canopy block a thermal camera, and a pet that keeps moving can leave the search area. A search covers ground much faster than walking it, but it does not guarantee your pet is found.

Can the Drone See Into Homes?

No. A thermal camera reads the surface heat of roofs and walls. It cannot see inside a building. The camera stays on the places your pet is most likely to be.
