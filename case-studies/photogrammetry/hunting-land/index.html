Case study · Hunting land

Read the ground
the way deer use it.

Good fit for Landowners Hunting leases & clubs Land buyers Habitat & food plot planning

On a wooded ridge at Enchanted Valley Acres, just north of Cross Plains, Wisconsin, we used a bare-earth elevation model to map the terrain features hunters usually spend seasons learning on foot: where water would drain and cool air tends to move, where the flat resting spots hide on steep slopes, and which draws connect the timber to the fields.All of it mapped from the air before you ever walk in.

What the map shows

Four things to look for

Estimated thermalsEstimated from slope direction and sun exposure: which slopes warm first and pull air uphill, and where cool air drains down at dawn. Use it to judge where your scent is likely to go, and which stands to hunt when.
Modeled drainage & drawsWhere surface water would flow, traced from the ground model. These are drainage pathways, not streams. They mark the draws deer travel between ridge and field, and the low ground that stays wet after rain.
BenchesFlat shelves on steep slopes, found automatically from the slope of the ground. Deer bed and travel on them, and they are easy to miss from the ground under full canopy.
Stands & accessPut it together to plan stand sites and quiet entry routes from a measured map instead of a guess.

Drainage paths and benches are calculated from the bare-earth elevation model. Thermal arrows are an estimate from terrain and sun exposure, not a measurement of air movement; wind and weather on a given day will override them. Under dense tree cover the ground model is partly estimated, so confirm features in heavy timber on foot.

Also available

More we can map from the same flights

Ridges & saddlesRidgelines, saddles, and the low crossings between them, classified automatically from the elevation model. Saddles are natural funnels for deer moving between ridges.
Conifer vs. hardwood coverFlown in late fall, when evergreens stay green and hardwoods have turned or dropped, the imagery separates the two so you can see where the cover is, block by block.
Probable oak standsOaks hold their brown leaves later into fall than most hardwoods, so likely oak stands can be flagged from the air and confirmed on the ground. A drone can't tell you how many acorns a tree will drop.
