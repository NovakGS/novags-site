Putting a Drone Map to the Test: Inside an ASPRS Drone Mapping Accuracy Test

A drone map can look sharp and still be in the wrong place. The only way to know is to check it against the ground, the same way the engineering world checks every other map. This is the story of one full check: 39 targets, 8,317 photos, a few things that went sideways, and the score at the end.

Why Test Drone Mapping Accuracy at All?

Most drone maps are sold on how they look: how sharp, how fast, how many acres. Those things matter. But when a contractor bills from a stockpile volume, or an engineer designs from an elevation, the question changes from “does it look right?” to “is it right, and by how much?”

Engineers, surveyors, and public agencies already have an answer for that. It is the ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards, published by the American Society for Photogrammetry and Remote Sensing. It spells out how to test a map, how many test points to use, and how to report the result.

Nothing requires a drone company to follow it. That is exactly why doing it says something.

Why Publish the Whole Process?

Accuracy claims are easy to make and hard to check from the outside. That’s why Novak Geospatial Solutions™ publishes this test in full: the method, every checkpoint, the problems, and the parts that are still open, like the survey-mark height question. Leaving the snags out would have made a cleaner story and a less useful one.

The intent is simple. An engineer, contractor, or landowner deciding whether to rely on drone data shouldn’t have to take anyone’s word for it. With the full results on the accuracy page, anyone can read the numbers, check the math, and see exactly what was proven and what wasn’t. Trust is earned by showing the work, including the parts that didn’t go to plan.

Four Terms, in Plain English

Ground Control Points (GCPs) – The Anchors
Targets on the ground, surveyed with GPS, that the software uses to pin the map in place.
Checkpoints – The Grading Key
Targets surveyed the same way but hidden from the software, then used to grade the finished map.
RMSE – The Typical Miss
Root mean square error: one number for how far off the map is across all the checkpoints. Lower is better.
Accuracy Class – The Standard to Meet
The RMSE a map is tested against. This test used 0.16 ft (5 cm) horizontal and 0.20 ft (6 cm) vertical.

  3. 03FlyOne RTK drone mission, 175 ft up, 8,317 photos.
  4. 04Build the MapUsing the 5 anchors only. Checkpoints stay hidden.
  5. 05Grade ItCompare the map to all 33 checkpoints.

A check on the check. The ground survey had to be graded too. Hours after the first pass, seven of the targets were surveyed a second time on a fresh GPS fix. Comparing the two readings showed the survey repeated within 0.04 ft horizontally and vertically, well inside the 0.08 ft and 0.10 ft the standard allows for a checkpoint survey at these accuracy classes.

The test site was an active gravel pit in Dane County, Wisconsin, flown on November 18, 2026. Open, bare ground with sand piles, gravel, a pond, and steep edges: a realistic site, not a parking lot.

The Ground Survey Comes First

Every target was surveyed with a GPS rover on a 2-meter pole, held perfectly upright by a bipod with its tip on the target’s center. Each reading was a 30-second average, accepted only on a fixed RTK solution, the most precise kind.

Before and after the field work, the rover was checked on a National Geodetic Survey mark, a bronze disk with a published position. Hours later, some targets were surveyed a second time to measure how repeatable the survey itself was. The standard requires that: the measuring stick has to be at least twice as accurate as the thing it measures.

What Went Wrong (And What Was Done About It)

No test goes perfectly. The standard doesn’t ask for a perfect day. It asks that every problem be documented and that no point be thrown out just because it looked bad. Here is the honest list.

1. The Software Marked Six Targets on Their Edges

Automatic target detection placed six marks on the edge of the target instead of the center, about a foot off. At this level of accuracy, a foot is a mile.

Fix: each one was re-marked on the true center by hand and checked before the final processing run.

2. One Processing Setting Lifted the Whole Map About 0.2 ft

The first run gave the drone’s own GPS about as much weight as the surveyed anchors. The result looked fine but sat about 0.2 ft high everywhere.

Fix: the software was set to trust the surveyed anchors, and the map dropped into place. It’s the kind of error that hides unless there are checkpoints to catch it.

3. The Wind Folded a Target

Checkpoint CP01 was surveyed flat in the morning. By the time the drone passed over, wind had folded it over. It was still folded at pickup.

Fix: CP01 was excluded for that physical reason, with this photo as proof. That left 33 checkpoints, still more than the 30 the standard requires.

4. Most of the Afternoon Re-Shots Didn’t Save

Targets were surveyed a second time in the afternoon to check repeatability, but only eight of those re-shots made it into the saved project. One was the folded CP01.

Fix: the survey’s repeatability was measured from the seven good pairs, and the report says so plainly.

5. The Survey Mark Disagreed on Height

Horizontally, every reading on the published mark matched within 0.053 ft, scattered in different directions. In height, every reading came in low, by 0.04 to 0.16 ft, and three were past the 0.098 ft the standard allows. Misses that always go the same way point to a consistent offset somewhere, and the cause isn’t settled yet.

Fix: rather than claim more than the data proves, the formal vertical accuracy statement is on hold. More published marks will be checked, and the report will be updated with the answer.

6. The Map Leaned Slightly Northwest

On average, the map placed checkpoints about 0.06 ft (under an inch) northwest of their surveyed spots, mostly at points surveyed early in the morning.

Fix: the standard calls for a lean like this to be investigated and reported. It was, and the full review is in the report. No correction was applied to make the numbers look better.

One more: a final review of the finished report caught a math error in a what-if calculation. It was corrected before publishing. Checking the checker is part of the process.

What This Means for Your Project

A test like this isn’t run on every job; it’s run once a year and whenever the main equipment or software changes. What carries over to every measured project is the method: checkpoints the software never sees, surveyed the same way, reported with the data. Jobs that need horizontal accuracy get at least 5 checkpoints. Jobs that need elevations get at least 5 ground control points and 5 checkpoints. You can see what that looks like in the sample project report, and how accuracy requirements fit into a budget in what drone mapping costs.

A drone and good software can make a map. Checking that map against the industry’s own standard is what turns it into data someone can build on.

Plenty of maps don’t need that proof: a marketing photo, a quick look at a property. But when the numbers drive a bid, a pay application, or a design, the check is the difference between a picture and a measurement. It is a step any drone mapping company can take. It just takes the time to do it and the willingness to publish what comes back.

Common Questions About Drone Mapping Accuracy

How Accurate Is Drone Mapping?

It depends on the equipment, the site, and how the work is checked. In this ASPRS test, a DJI Matrice 4E RTK workflow with 5 ground control points measured 0.10 ft (3.0 cm) horizontal RMSE and 0.09 ft (2.6 cm) vertical RMSE on open, bare ground. Vegetation, steep faces, and water reduce what is achievable.

What Is the ASPRS Accuracy Standard?

The ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards for Digital Geospatial Data, from the American Society for Photogrammetry and Remote Sensing, set out how to test and report the accuracy of maps and elevation data. Edition 2 (2024) is the current version used by engineers, surveyors, and public agencies.

What Is the Difference Between a Ground Control Point and a Checkpoint?

A ground control point is a surveyed target the software uses to position the map. A checkpoint is surveyed the same way but kept out of processing, so it can be used to grade the finished map independently.

Does Every Drone Mapping Project Need an Accuracy Test?

A full ASPRS test isn’t needed on every job, but measured projects should still be checked. Novak|GS uses at least 5 independent checkpoints when horizontal accuracy matters, and at least 5 ground control points plus 5 checkpoints when elevations matter, with the results delivered alongside the data.
